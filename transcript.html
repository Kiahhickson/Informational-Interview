01Topic

Introduction

Kiah

Hi John, thank you for doing this interview with me. You’ve known me through a significant part of my career, and you even encouraged me to go to university, so I thought you’d be a particularly good person to speak with about what comes next now that I’m at the tail end of that. As you know, my last assignment is due on Thursday and I’ll be graduating in December.

Longer term, I want to understand what broader digital and transformation leadership, and eventually executive roles in the public sector, might look like for me and how I can get there. Given your experience as a senior leader, and now in executive recruitment, I’d really value your perspective on where I’m at, what you’re seeing in the market and what I should be doing to develop. Before we start, are you comfortable with me recording our conversation?

John

Yes, sure.

02Topic

Trends in public-sector executive recruitment

Kiah

In terms of trends, what are you seeing in public-sector executive recruitment at the moment, particularly around digital, data, AI and transformation? Are more roles coming up, and what are those roles expecting from senior people?

John

Firstly, congratulations on getting this far. I knew you would do it, and I’m proud of you. Well done.

Kiah

Thank you. Final piece of the puzzle.

John

I might go back slightly in history. It wasn’t that long ago that the public service was very independent. It was set up that way intentionally. We used to talk about providing “frank and fearless advice” to whoever was in government. That has been changing for about 30 years. The very senior levels of the public service have become more politicised, so I’m not sure all of those very senior appointments are made purely on merit. It trickles down a little, but at the levels you’re looking at, you should still be largely clear of that.

Kiah

So are you saying some of these newer roles go to people with more of a public profile rather than the experience to get the job done?

John

Not exactly, but there are flaws in every system. It used to be that if you stayed long enough, and you were good enough, you’d end up as the Director-General after 40 years in the public service. Now a Director-General is more likely to come in towards the end of their career because they’re connected to, or share the views of, the government of the day. That never used to matter. Of course, the old way brought problems too, because the person who’s been there the longest isn’t necessarily the best, so some of that had to change. We now have a much more presidential-style system in both Commonwealth and state public services. I do a lot of work in local government and haven’t seen it as much there yet, because politics tends not to play as big a role.

Most executive roles now go through a recruitment agency. Organisations rarely do it themselves anymore, partly for resourcing and partly for independence.

At the next level for you, in the digital environment, you’re much better qualified than me to understand how that works. But from what I’m seeing, executive recruitment is changing very rapidly. I’d estimate 75% of the applications and cover letters I receive are probably written by AI. Not long ago there would have been none. So it’s harder to separate the wheat from the chaff, and you have to dig deeper. I think the CV is becoming more important than the cover letter. People want to see where you’ve been, what you’ve done and for how long.

Tenure is an interesting one. Not long ago, moving every two years was frowned upon. Some of my more traditional clients still care about it, but regular movement is much more mainstream now, especially in the circles I move in. Because things have become politicised, many council CEOs lose their jobs when a new mayor comes in, so you can’t always blame the candidate. Employers still like to see some stability, though. If you’re moving every six months, they’ll ask why you’re not getting past probation. It used to be that five years or more was the benchmark. Now it’s probably two or three, and people don’t take as much notice.

Kiah

You mainly recruit CEOs and general managers in finance or operations. Have you seen more IT or cyber roles coming through?

John

It depends on the size of the client. Cyber tends to be outsourced quite a lot to specialist companies that keep up with what’s trending. Even bigger councils don’t have many cyber experts. They have some cyber skills, but they rely heavily on outside expertise, which makes sense because it’s a very specialist area.

Digital is a bit different. Organisations want to control the story and the messaging themselves if they have the experience and expertise. And “digital” is being interpreted much more widely. It’s not just the council’s ERP system. There’s a big focus on customers. We’ve all talked about being customer-centric. It used to be called user-centric, and the computer companies didn’t like that so much, so you tended to get what you got. Now organisations are genuinely interested in good data. They know they have a lot of good information, but they don’t know how to pick it up and use it.

Kiah

So if you were looking at a candidate for a more senior operational role who had a data analytics background, or could show in their application that they can interpret data, what impression would that give you?

John

Generally positive. The more senior you get, the more you need those skills so the wool doesn’t get pulled over your eyes. The very senior public service used to be full of generalists who didn’t specialise in one area but grew up in the system. The higher you go, the less operational and the more strategic you become. We’ve all seen the matrix: a team member might be 90% operational and 10% strategic. By the time you’re a senior executive it’s probably 70/30 the other way. You never escape the operational, but you need those core skills to do the strategic work properly, because you need to know what’s missing and how to put it all together. So having runs on the board in a digital environment is very important, especially if you’ve kept up your training.

03Topic

What makes someone ready for the next level

Kiah

When someone senior is looking to jump to Director or executive level, what distinguishes a person who is genuinely ready to make that move?

John

That’s a good question. For me it’s partly client-based, because every client wants something different. Candidates often ask me what they could have done better, and sometimes I say, “I sat through that interview and you did really well. I probably would have chosen you, but the client saw something else.”

So I’d say research the decision-makers. Find out as much as you can about who they are, what makes them tick and what drives them, and target your response to that. If there’s something important they want that you don’t have, find a way to sell what you do have. Don’t run away from the gap. Explain how you could get it, how you could apply what you have, or what in your history answers that question for them.

In the final stages it can become a bit of a personality contest, because it’s about the connection and the right fit for the organisation. And it’s a two-way street. Clients sometimes think that once they’ve picked someone that’s the end of it, but they have to work for the candidate as much as the candidate works for them. If you’re asking people to pack up their lives, move and leave something more stable, there has to be respect both ways.

Kiah

Have you successfully recruited someone from a senior role into their first Director or executive role? What did that look like, and why did they get the position?

John

Absolutely. Firstly, you have to find people who want to step up, because a lot of people get comfortable and they see the exposure that comes with senior roles. In local government, there are many directors who don’t want to be CEO because the extra money isn’t worth the pressure and stress. You have to be driven to want that, and it’s not for everybody.

I’ve pushed people to be promoted and it didn’t work. I challenged myself on why. They were really good at their job and I thought they’d be good at the next one, but I was wrong. They weren’t good at leading or managing people, and they were uncomfortable doing it.

Kiah

So know your lane.

John

Yes. Clients often tell me they want someone with CEO experience, and I say, “Just open your mind to someone who hasn’t had it.” I say this a fair bit: the very best CEO in the state was once a really good director, and before that a really good manager, and before that a really good principal or team leader. I’ve lived it myself, moving through all of those phases. You could miss out on the very best candidate if you don’t keep an open mind.

Kiah

What would give you the confidence that someone is the right person to step up?

John

How they present in their application, their resume, their interview and their conversations with me.

Kiah

What about education and qualifications?

John

It depends on the job. A finance manager probably needs accounting qualifications. An infrastructure director probably needs engineering qualifications.

Kiah

What about communications or digital?

John

That’s harder. The pyramid gets much narrower at the top. I used to have 2,000 employees and I’d tell them they can’t all be the CEO. Mathematically it’s not going to happen, even if everyone wanted it. Finance and engineering have probably had more opportunities, but that doesn’t mean there haven’t been success stories from other streams.

Because digital is changing so rapidly, it’s becoming a much more prominent skill set. I sit on some audit committees and work with others, and at the moment everything I read says they want people with digital skills. Not long ago they all wanted project management or accounting skills. In quite a lot of cases, digital is now one of the top things they’re looking for. Cyber matters because it’s a risk, but so does the broader digital picture. The communications part is important because you need to get your message out, but you might not want to stop there. You want to go to the next level.

I’d also talk about having a good attitude and acumen over experience. You can teach people how to do things, but it’s very hard to teach a good attitude or natural acumen. You have to be confident but not cocky.

04Topic

Personal brand and authenticity

John

People also have different brands, and it depends on what you’re going into. Some clients tell me the place is a mess and they want a disruptor. That’s a particular type of person. Disruptors can get things done quickly, but they don’t usually last, because people can’t handle their pace and the level of change. That’s their brand: they come in, fix it and move on. Other times a client just wants a safe pair of hands because things are going well, they don’t want any trouble and they have good support at director level. They need a leader, a mentor and a coach, not necessarily a player.

Kiah

How can someone on the outside best understand what an organisation is going through? Sometimes it’s very public, especially in local government, and you can read the news. And would it help to present yourself a certain way in your cover letter, for example as a safe pair of hands? If an ad says they want a rock star, you might assume they want a disruptor. If a council says it just wants someone to keep the wheels turning, do you lean into that? What if you just want the opportunity and you’re willing to adapt your personal brand to get your foot in the door? Is that a good strategic move, or will it end in tears?

John

More likely end in tears. I’m big on this: try to be as authentic and genuine as you can. I know it’s a game and you probably have to talk yourself up a little as part of the process. But if the recruiter or organisation is doing their job properly, they’ll have tried to get the message out about the type of person they want. You can say you’re that person, but it’s much better if you genuinely are.

If you say you’re going to come in and clean things up, you need to be comfortable making very difficult decisions. At that level everyone has to make tough decisions, but some people are quite happy to be, for want of a better word, mean, and a lot of people just don’t like doing that. If you say you’ll do it, and six months later they say you haven’t done any of it, you probably won’t last and you won’t be happy. You’ll be uncomfortable. So find what’s as close as possible to who you genuinely are, and sell that.

A lot of people also waste their time. Clients will specify things that candidates don’t have, and candidates apply anyway, then ask me why they weren’t successful. I tell them, “We told you up front that you needed A, B and C, and you don’t have any of them. The people you were up against did.”

Moving up internally can be a good way to go. If you have a good reputation, you can be the good principal, the good manager, the good director, and find your way to the top. That might take patience because you might miss out once or twice, so you have to hang in there. It’s hard to fake it internally because people know you. But if you’re doing a really good job, that’s more than half the battle.

05Topic

How I have developed

Kiah

You knew me when I was at Redland City Council, and I was there for six years. You’ve seen a lot of my development during that time and since. Where do you think I’ve developed the most?

John

A couple of things. The easy one first: technically. I’m on the outside looking in now, but you’re producing better quality outcomes because of your skill set. You’re ambitious, and you know strategy is important, so you go out of your way to be as strategic as you can in your role.

As a person, I see you take leading other people seriously. You’re very sharing and welcoming. You’re not tough, and I’m not saying you need to be. You have a presence that doesn’t need that, because you can still be honest and objective and give balanced feedback in a caring and genuine way.

Personal maturity is a big one. That needle has shifted a lot. I don’t think you did that for your career. I think you did it for you: for want of a better word, growing up. But it’s something you needed to do to be on the path you’re on and want to be on. Without it, we wouldn’t be having this conversation, because you wouldn’t be at uni. You might have gone down a more alternative path. I know you love startups and new things, and that could have been successful too, with hard yards. But you now have a much better percentage bet on your future by taking a more balanced approach.

06Topic

What to lean into

Kiah

Is there anything in my career so far that you think I should lean into more?

John

You’ve settled on digital, but that means different things to different people. For some it means social media, for some it means ERP systems, for some it means AI. Not long ago there was no such thing as a chief digital officer. Now it’s a mainstream term, and those officers do different things in different organisations even with the same title. It’s definitely becoming more encompassing. To me, it’s about the future of technology, and that applies differently to different organisations.

Kiah

I agree. What we traditionally saw as public affairs or media has shifted as digital platforms become a priority channel for communicating. Depending on the organisation, a chief digital officer might be more technical, focused on AI, cyber security and digital systems. In other organisations it may be more about communications, customer experience and continuous improvement.

John

It’ll find its way and mature. I’ve been around long enough to remember people talking about the paperless office while we burned through paper. You couldn’t just declare it and make it happen, but people grew into it over time. Now, for a newer generation, printing something out barely enters your mind. Your home office doesn’t even need a printer. That changed in a very short time.

07Topic

Gaps to address

Kiah

Based on what you know about me and where I ultimately want to be, what gaps should I address before I seriously start targeting Director or executive roles?

John

A couple of things. If you just want to move, you can shotgun applications. But I’d rather see you take a more targeted approach. You won’t know the answer yet, and there isn’t a straight answer, but roughly where do you want to go? Do you want to stay in state government? Would you go back to local government?

I’d also take a break from study once you finish this degree, and maybe think about yourself. There are all sorts of management, leadership and psychometric profiling tools. Some of them can be confronting, when they tell you “this is you” and you think, “That’s not really me.” Sometimes you take it with a grain of salt, but it comes back to knowing yourself: your strengths and your weaknesses, and trying to turn those weaknesses into positives, even if they don’t become strengths.

Kiah

So for me, I’m technical enough, I’m on the tools enough, and I’ll have this degree, so I’m academically qualified. I’ve done a lot of extracurricular learning over the last couple of years, like software development and human-centred design, to build my skills and resume. But what I’m hearing is that at the next level it’s more about people leadership, a strategic mindset, emotional intelligence and relationships with peers. What are the natural ways to build the things that make a really good Director, CEO or leader?

John

I was lucky to work with people who invested in me. I had so much leadership training and support available that it was almost ridiculous, but it was very helpful. If you don’t have that, you might have to go and get it yourself. I used to work for a CEO who read all the books and listened to podcasts on his way to work. He took it very seriously. When you finish your degree, I’m not saying be as fanatical as him, but instead of watching a show, give yourself the time on the bus to listen to a leadership podcast.

08Topic

The Army Reserve

Kiah

I’ve shared that I’ve applied for the Army Reserve. Do you think that would be a good way to build leadership, discipline and focus? I’ve barely finished this degree and I’m already jumping into something else, although it’s more experiential learning than academic.

John

Yes and no. You don’t want to be a full-time military person, but the military has a reputation for providing really good training in whatever they train you in, including discipline. It’s a bit like the degree. It shows application, to the community and to yourself, and the extracurricular effort you’re willing to put in. It certainly wouldn’t be a negative. It’s another story you can tell about leadership and teamwork, which are pretty important to employers.

Kiah

Let’s say the new year comes and I get a Director role and join the Reserve at the same time. How would you see me managing my time? I tend to jump in the deep end and figure it out, and knowing me, everything will happen at once.

John

I think you have a high capacity to take things on. I hadn’t thought of this until just now, but if you get a Director role, that’s probably the most important part of the journey at this moment. Don’t be afraid to modify the Reserve commitment, because the Director role is probably better for your longer-term security. The Reserve is flexible and you might be able to make it work. But if you have to choose one, you’d choose the Director job.

Kiah

I’m also thinking about how it would look on my resume. There’s the service to the community and to Australia, the training and the leadership. I’m being strategic about it: I get the experience, I get paid, and it’s tax free. But from a resume point of view, is it an interesting, unique thing that a recruiter might notice?

John

Yes, it’s a point of difference. I know exactly what you’re doing, and it wouldn’t be a negative. You’re not going there to fire missiles or dig trenches. You’re going into a specialist area.

Kiah

Public affairs.

John

Within the military. You and I both know from our disaster work how important that messaging is: for public safety, for the safety of your team, and for supporting the government, the military and everyone else. It’s a key role. It has to be done well. It has to be disciplined, structured, timely and accurate.

Kiah

It becomes the source of truth.

John

Absolutely. You have to take that seriously, because many people rely on the information you put out. So strategically it would be good. People won’t even need you to tell them. They’ll see “Army Reserve” and know it means training and leadership. They might only ask out of interest. And it gives them a different lens to view you through as an applicant.

09Topic

Gender and first impressions

Kiah

Maybe it’s also about not judging a book by its cover. You interview a lot of people, sometimes in regional and remote areas, and as a woman I think we’re judged a bit more on hair, makeup, outfits and handbags, for better or worse. The Army Reserve might make someone think, “She’s not that much of a princess if she can rummage around in the dirt.” That’s a sobering thing to say out loud, but is that the reality of recruitment?

John

It’s still the reality of the outcome of recruitment processes sometimes. I get clients who won’t say it out loud, but they want a man, for all the wrong reasons, and they’ll discount the female candidates. Most of them wouldn’t know anything about your handbag, but that element of sexism still exists in senior-level decision-making, and I see it more than I’d like to. It has changed a lot, but not as much as it needs to. There have been some really good pace-setters, and there are quite a lot of very successful female council CEOs and directors in state and federal government, so it’s chipping away. But women are 50% of the population and not 50% of those jobs, so we haven’t reached equality yet. I hope we do.

You’re very personable, so I think you’ll win people over on that level. You’ve put me on the spot a little, and I don’t know how we overcome what some people might see as shortcomings. I’m pretty honest with candidates that this is often what they’re up against. That’s why you sometimes have to be patient, because turning up for work every day becomes experience, and that can take a little longer than we’d all like. I know you’re ambitious, and I have no doubt you’ll get there. I don’t want to be negative, but it might take a little longer than you think, and that’s okay. I’m not going to put a damper on your energy and enthusiasm.

10Topic

Stretch assignments

Kiah

That’s a good segue. What roles, projects or extracurricular assignments would you deliberately pursue over the next two or three years if you were in my position?

John

You need to work for an employer that gives you those opportunities. My employer gave me lots of training, and my bosses were enormously supportive. They pushed me along, helped me and encouraged me.

I also started young and said no to nothing. I served beers at the mayor’s Friday night cocktail party, read water meters, picked up dead snakes, went to community events, and was basically the mayor’s driver for a couple of weeks. Whatever opportunity came up, I put my hand up. I grew out of that, but it sent a message.

I also had lots of relieving opportunities, and not necessarily in my area of expertise. Later on I became more of a generalist, a problem solver and a people manager, with a background in finance and economics. My main focus was always corporate, but I was in charge of planning, and I was in charge of engineering for a couple of years because it was a mess and they needed someone to sort it out. I wasn’t put there as a head-kicker. I had to make some tough decisions, but I always took a building and rebuilding approach rather than a blowing-up approach. It was a heavily unionised workforce, so realistically I couldn’t have just walked in and sacked everyone anyway, not that I wanted to.

If you can get those opportunities, take them. You’ve just done a secondment in state government, and that was a good opportunity.

Kiah

It was great.

John

It sends a message. I’m very confident that when you get an interview for one of these jobs in state government, you’ll tell the DPC (Department of the Premier and Cabinet) story, and it will be a selling point in your cover letter and your resume. So the Reserve, finishing the degree, and what you’re doing in your current role: say yes.

Kiah

You said you grew out of saying yes to everything. Was that because you didn’t need to prove yourself anymore? You were in the same organisation for a long time.

John

Thirty years.

Kiah

So by then, did people know you well enough to see you as a doer, a problem solver, someone who thinks critically, is good with people and is reliable?

John

Correct.

Kiah

A safe pair of hands. Is that how you’d describe yourself to an organisation?

John

No, I don’t really like that. I hate labels. When you’ve been somewhere for 30 years, the world changes. One week I’m a dinosaur and they should get rid of me. The next week I’m a wealth of knowledge and they couldn’t possibly part with me, because I know all the history and how it works. I never felt comfortable with labels. I loved the place. I was passionate about the people and the business, and I fitted in from day one. I still had to prove myself, and I missed out on plenty of jobs during my career, and got some. But you reach a point where people have set ideas about you and make different decisions, so you have to be resilient and a bit thick-skinned.

11Topic

Moving on to move up

Kiah

For younger people like me, the common advice is that if you want a pay rise and better opportunities, you have to leave every couple of years, because the same opportunities don’t come up in one organisation anymore. What do you think of that approach?

John

It becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy, doesn’t it? We’re labelling a generation as people who’ll have 15 different careers in a lifetime. I get that the world is changing, but there’ll still be the apprentice who starts at a big company and goes to uni. You have to step up yourself. Nothing is going to be handed to you on a platter, and it never was. People have always had to step up. And if people think moving every couple of years will fix everything, I’m not so sure.

Kiah

I was at council for six years and I had good opportunities. People invested in my development, I did short courses and then university, and I had acting roles and promotions. But there came a point where I felt I’d capped out and couldn’t get where I wanted in the time frame I wanted, so I moved, which also had lifestyle benefits. Now I’m feeling that again. I’ve been in state government for two years and there’s no clear succession path in my current area. I have a vision of where I’d like to get, and I’m on a mission to get there. The training and leadership development opportunities I’m looking for just aren’t there for me where I am now.

John

It can feel very impersonal once you’re in those big state agencies. Look, after five years in a role you’re a free agent. In this market, people wouldn’t even blink at that. They’d look at your resume and think, “She did five years there, that’s good,” and they’d see the progression.

You’re working with people now who are AO5s in the public service, and they’ll still be AO5s when they retire. Some have no ambition to go further. Some have ambition but don’t have the ability to back it up, and they won’t be successful. You’ve got ambition, but you’ve got ability to back it up. You just have to convince others of that through the process.

12Topic

Leading people

Kiah

What if they already know, but they don’t know what to do with it? What to do with a problem like Kiah? I feel like that’s more my barrier. I’m ambitious, capable and passionate.

John

You’ve worked for the wrong people. The goal of the good manager should be to hire people smarter than them.

Kiah

I believe that too. People who are better than you.

John

Yes. As you get more senior, your portfolio gets wider and you won’t be the subject matter expert in everything. As a leader you need to invest in your people, back them, and give them the direction and leadership they need to thrive in their area of expertise. Some will be problem children and cause you no end of grief, but not badly enough to move on, so you bring them with you. And wherever you work, people will have favourites, or someone will simply be the best at what they do.

I’ve done all sorts of leadership training and surveys. There’s often no right or wrong answer, but in how those things are measured, I would always have been near the top of the class. I’m not saying that to talk myself up. People would ask what I was doing to score that way in a 360 or personal evaluation, and I’d say, “I don’t know. I’m just doing what I do.” I look after my people, I support them, I work hard and I study hard. I think my biggest blessing is that I’ve had good intuition.

Kiah

So you trust your gut?

John

I was one of those people who could find something on page 78 and say, “That just doesn’t look right to me. I’m not going to solve the problem, but you’d better have a look at page 78 and convince me it’s not wrong.” They’d find a problem. And there’s a calmness about problem solving. A military-type example: the bombs are all going off and John’s just walking ahead. I can’t even hear them. It’s white noise. “This is where I want to get to. You keep doing that, but I’m not getting involved.”

13Topic

Psychometric testing and self-awareness

Kiah

We talked about psychometric evaluations and whether they’re useful. There are so many out there, like Myers-Briggs, and I know my personality types. I just did a psychometric test for the Defence Force and could see where I lean, although I haven’t had my results yet. When you have candidates do them, you probably have a type of person in mind for the job. What happens if someone looks right at first glance but their results say otherwise? How reliable are they, do candidates get their results, and what’s the value?

John

Sometimes I see the outcomes. I think people would be surprised how little emphasis gets placed on them, and I’m not saying that’s right. Picture a big Venn diagram with an average blob in the middle. Most people sit in the blob, and most people in the blob can find a way to manage their way through. It doesn’t raise red flags, and you’ll usually sail through. If you’re out in the other circles, that requires further investigation into that person’s personality type. Roughly 70% of people are in the blob. The other 30% are out on their own, sometimes exactly where the client wants them for a particular skill set.

It’s hard to change those outcomes over time, so it’s about knowing yourself and knowing your weaknesses. Not your academic skills, but your personality. What am I not good at? I recently worked with a CEO who didn’t need much recognition himself, but his people did, and because he didn’t need it, he never gave it. I said, “If you’re not going to do it, get someone who will. Fill the gap. Have a conversation with your HR manager. Tell them you’re not good at recognition and ask them to drag you along. Get them to organise a morning tea to thank Kiah and make you come.” He knows he’s not good at it, but his people need it. That’s not something that would stop him getting the CEO job, but it is understanding yourself. When you have that realisation, even if you don’t personally do better, you get better outcomes. People are more satisfied, they like the recognition, they work harder, and you get productivity for nothing.

Kiah

That comes back to the classic interview question about your weaknesses, where everyone says they’re a perfectionist. What you’re saying is that if you really know yourself and are honest about it, leadership isn’t about being all things to everyone. It’s about finding the right people to help build a better team culture.

John

I used to do this with my team. They were all different. Some professions or backgrounds come with a reputation, and I had people working for me whose first language wasn’t English, so people formed impressions that were probably wrong, and we had to make that work. I was looking for balance. I didn’t want everyone in my team to be the same. It was about understanding the differences in the team so we could support each other and communicate in the way each person needed. You need someone strategic, someone in the detail, and someone high on emotional intelligence who gives people cuddles, metaphorically. Imagine all of those things coming to the table. History says you’ll make better decisions. A team should be looking for where you can fill its gaps.

Looking back

Looking back, what do you know now about leadership and career progression that you wish you’d understood earlier?

John

That’s hard to answer, because I feel I got pretty lucky and I’m happy with how it turned out. If I’d told my younger self that this would be the endgame, I would have considered myself very successful.

I’d agree. Do you feel you reached your leadership positions at the right time, or do you wish they’d come sooner?

John

I think the timing was generally pretty good. I missed out on jobs I thought I should have got, and looking back, I probably wasn’t ready. Sometimes I was wrong about that too. Perseverance matters. I had to work hard and put in extra effort, but I probably did more than I needed to.

15Topic

AI and authenticity

John

During this interview I’ve been thinking that everything is becoming so fake. Your question about the right answer to interview questions triggered me. People are going to ChatGPT and asking what questions they’ll be asked and what the right answers are, and then they give that answer even though it’s a complete fabrication of what they’d actually do. I had lunch with someone involved in AI in Australia, and he said there’ll be work for people like me to sort through it all, because some candidates will never have seen, produced or done the thing in practice. They’ll be relying on someone or something else. It’s going to be very hard to find out who the real people are in recruitment.

Kiah

So internal candidates may become more valuable, because at least you know them. And there’s bias too. Someone charismatic can talk the talk and give the ideal answer with no real idea, while someone else has the substance but isn’t as extroverted.

John

Sadly, that happens every day. It usually ends in tears, because they can’t keep it up day to day. It’s not genuine, because it’s not them. But yes, there are people getting jobs purely on talk, and it’s not a good thing.

Kiah

So that’s where authenticity comes through, and maybe unique experiences like the Army Reserve help someone seem more interesting, so an interviewer wants to know more.

John

It says you’ve given up some of your weekends and you’re committed to a greater good. Strategically, it could be a good thing.

16Topic

Study or experience

John

I wouldn’t do any more study at the moment, but eventually you might have to.

Kiah

You did an MBA while working, with your workplace’s support. Should I consider an MBA as part of my next step? Would it strengthen my position for executive roles, or should I prioritise more experience first?

John

It’ll be both. I just don’t want you to do it right now, in the very short term. It would be good if you could work somewhere that would pay for or sponsor it. When we talked about generalist skills, employers don’t want you to be an accountant, but in senior roles you have to manage budgets, people and resources. That’s leadership, finance and budgeting, and that’s what an MBA is likely to focus on, because it’s a gap for professionals of every kind. Accountants, economists, engineers and health professionals running hospitals trained to be experts in their field, not leaders of 1,000 people, but that’s where they end up. They need that training, but ideally we’d find a way for yours to be sponsored.

Kiah

So maybe in the future, but not right now. Let’s see how far I can progress in the next year or two. If I can secure a position without it, that would be my preference, unless it’s a specific tech-focused MBA.

John

You do love your tech. What about graduate certificates? They’re much shorter and you can squeeze them in. So eventually, but not right now.

Realistically, if we’re staying in state government and the right Director opportunity came up, that’s your long-term goal too, but what would you do after that?

Kiah

Executive Director. Chief Digital Officer.

John

So there are other steps in that process. I’m not saying don’t look for those opportunities, and we wouldn’t dismiss them, but the next level up from where you are is probably the next safe step.

Kiah

I agree. I feel the only way I’d get higher than that is through a warm lead, where someone in that department knew me, my work standard and what I’m capable of, and supported me.

John

I agree, and you haven’t got that just yet. That can take time. Some of the people you work with have grown up in that system. You haven’t. You’re relatively new, and it takes a while.

17Topic

Building the right network

Kiah

If you were me and wanted to be well placed for the next big move over the next two to three years, what would you be doing now?

Relationships and networking. I’m not saying be best friends with these people. People say “friendly, but not friends”. I don’t know enough about it now, but there used to be groups. I think it was the Institute of Public Administration, where public servants got together and learned about being good public servants. I don’t know if they’re still around. You’re already doing some of this through your secondment, so you’re building those relationships. It’s all online these days, so do a bit of research and see what networks are out there.

Kiah

I think that’s a gap for me. In the tech scene in Sydney it was very easy to network. There were always events and meetups, and I took full advantage of that. I miss that supportive, collaborative environment where everyone was willing to help, make new friends and share their knowledge. I haven’t had the same experience in government. Now that I’m finishing the degree, I have more mental bandwidth, and I live and work closer to the city, so it’s something I should focus on over the next few months and into next year.

John

There used to be lunchtime sessions somewhere.

Kiah

Everything is online now, but I really like meeting people in person and getting to know them.

John

I was lucky that local government is a very sharing environment. You’re not competing with those people, so I didn’t have to go out of my way. I got involved in LGMA. It was one conference a year, but you got to know people and then used each other as a resource during the year.

Kiah

Exactly. I was even invited to speak at those conferences, because the person whose job I took at council supported me. He was kind enough to say, “She’s got good experience, let’s get her on stage.” I loved that about local government.

Closing reflections

Kiah

I think you’re a girl in a hurry.

I did 30 years, so at this age I was probably a manager, if we talk in those levels. I didn’t know much about anything, but I grew into it. It just might not happen on your timeline, because it’s not entirely in your control.

Kiah

In my head, I’m 35 next year and I’d like to be a manager or Director by now.

I’d think right now you should be a manager.

And I’m not quite there yet.

But you’re only one step below.

I know, but I see people at those levels and wonder how they got there. It can feel like it’s more about who you know than what you know, and that’s frustrating.

I keep going back and forth on this, because I don’t want you to be frustrated by it. There are people who probably shouldn’t have got those jobs. Probably, yes.

So that’s a good thing, because it gives us hope. We don’t focus on the ones who shouldn’t be there. We focus on the ones who’ve done the hard yards. And you could always go to the private sector. You’re not tied to government.

I know, but I like the safety net. I’ve almost got my long service leave, and I like the general benefits of the public sector.

These are the compromises we make. I’m very confident in your ability, and I’m very confident you’ll be successful by your own definition, because it’s really about you. What I’ve taken away from this is that you need the technical aptitude, but what you’ve really been telling me is that it’s about who you are as a person: your attitude, your leadership skills and whether you’re a people person. Think about the managers who worked for me. We had 2,000 people and 30 managers, so they were in the top 30 out of a couple of thousand. That’s pretty good, and they were really good at their jobs. And that’s why people reach a level where they say, “Actually, this is enough for me.” That happens. I know plenty of people who are happy to stay in the public service at level 7 or 8 for the rest of their careers, reviewing contracts, because that makes them happy and comfortable.

I haven’t reached that point yet. I think there’s more exploring and achieving I need to do to figure out where that threshold is. Thank you for your time, John.
